<!--

I wanted to start with the classic opening question. But I need to clarify a few things first, just to be sure we’re all on the same page.

So, here’s some definitions.

I use the expression *agentic factory* to describe some kind of setup where AI agents work with various degrees of autonomy throughout the software delivery (or development) lifecycle, aka SDLC.

That is not software developers prompting Claude Code, GitHub Copilot, or another coding assistant to get their code generated by an LLM. An agentic factory is something much more structured than that.

And an agentic factory is not what people call *vibe coding*. A vibe coder is someone who wakes up on a weekend with little or no knowledge of software engineering and, most of all, with nothing better to do, and uses a coding assistant to create some sort of software application. By the way, every time I hear about vibe coding, I have this sort of allergic reaction. And I wonder why anyone has ever come up with the idea of vibe surgery.

Back to the factory. You might also have heard the ominous expression *dark factory*. That refers to a setup where automation is pushed to the point where human labor or presence is no longer required. In these environments, agents refine, plan, code, test, and potentially merge code into production.

That is a vision that gives me the creeps. And that should tell you something, because I’m a huge fan of everything horror and science fiction, and therefore not easily impressed.

# Architecture

<br />

![](/factory-architecture.jpg)

<!--

This is my attempt at summarizing its architecture in a diagram.

There’s this custom web app, frontend and backend, that is supposed to be used by the engineering teams to create jobs for the factory.

Consider that this system was conceived to be used by several development teams working on thousands of different projects.

The frontend allows engineers to select tasks from Jira, bundle them into batches, and let the factory work on them.

Jira is where the business analysts write the requirements.

The vision was that engineers, at the end of their day, would create a batch of tasks, turn on the factory, and go home.

Between the backend of the custom web app and the workers there’s a simple asynchronous layer based on queues and events.

Most of this infrastructure runs on AWS.

When a job is picked up, a containerized worker is started. Inside it, Claude Code runs in non-interactive mode, aka headless mode.

Headless is another expression that gives me the creeps.

So, I imagine Claude here as the Headless Horseman from *The Legend of Sleepy Hollow*.

And the Headless Horseman rides to Jira to retrieve the specification, then to GitLab to check out the repository, then to the code graph to explore the structure and relationships in the codebase.

The graph is built by ingesting the source code with CodeGraphContext and storing the resulting relationships in a graph database. The graph provides the agents with structured project context, so they don’t have to rediscover everything from the codebase every time.

But agents can’t do much without models. And here comes LiteLLM, acting as a gateway between the Headless Horseman and the models hosted on Amazon Bedrock.

Why have an LLM gateway in the middle? Because it gives you one controlled entry point for model access. Authentication. Budgets. Guardrails. Logging. Especially in large organizations, this centralization is crucial.

Then there’s another gateway for tools. Amazon Bedrock AgentCore Gateway exposes approved tools via MCP, so instead of letting agents connect arbitrarily to anything and everything, you can give them controlled access to Jira, GitLab, and web search.

And around all this you need observability. And AgentCore integrates with CloudWatch and OpenTelemetry-compatible telemetry.

So, the Headless Horseman uses its skills and templates to refine, plan, code, test, and eventually create a merge request in GitLab.

And the morning after, the engineer wakes up, drinks a coffee, and starts reviewing code.

-->

# Challenges

<br />

::left::

- The human bottleneck

<v-clicks>

- Auditability + identity attribution

  - Who created the merge request?
  - Who added the comment to Jira?

::right::

<v-clicks>

- No definition of success
  -	Faster code?

  -	More backlog items completed?
  -	Fewer defects?
  -	Less review effort?

</v-clicks>

<!--

The first thought that came to mind when we understood the architecture was that this thing produces code way faster than any human can possibly review.

But the risk of a human bottleneck was not the main reason why the factory was stuck somewhere between PoC and MVP and was not being adopted.

My colleague and I identified two major design flaws. And a third, non-technical problem.

First, the factory had not been designed to meet the auditability and accountability requirements of a highly regulated environment.

You all know how a PoC or MVP is typically developed. Requirements are sacrificed to meet the deadline. Especially non-functional requirements. And in this case, some of the most critical. Identity attribution. Delegation. Authorization. Auditability. In a highly regulated environment, you need to be able to answer questions from auditors.

- Who wrote that code?
- Who created that merge request?
- Who added that comment to Jira?
- What were the roles and permissions of the Headless Horseman?
- Under whose authority was he acting?
- Who executed that *drop database* command in production?

Second, the factory had not been designed with the proper guardrails in place, particularly around the handling of personally identifiable and highly confidential information.

And, more generally, around the risks connected to the unpredictable nature of the Headless Horseman. Because we don’t know what prompts he will send to the models.

That’s one of the reasons why there’s an LLM gateway. Fine. But no one had sat down with the security folks and conducted a proper gap analysis against the compliance requirements. No one had conducted a proper risk analysis.

The third problem we identified wasn’t technical, but nonetheless crucial to adoption. No one had defined how to measure success. What are the goals?

- Faster code?
- More code?
- Lower cost?
- More backlog items completed?
- Fewer defects?
- Less review effort?

Without a baseline and a definition of success, you end up not even knowing whether the factory works.

-->

# Main focus

SPIFFE ( https://spiffe.io ) + SPIRE

-	Getting rid of system accounts and shared personal access tokens.
-	Introducing delegated and on-behalf-of identity patterns, currently experimenting with Microsoft Entra Agent ID and agent identity blueprints.
-	Propagating identity attribution beyond the gateways, so that when an agent performs an action on GitLab, Jira, or another system, we can still establish who initiated it and under whose authority it happened.
-	Allowing agents to retrieve current information from the web through an AgentCore Web Search Tool instead of simply giving them unrestricted internet access.
-	Sitting in long meetings with the security folks discussing guardrails, prompt filtering, personally identifiable and highly confidential information, and what may or may not end up in prompts and logs.
-	And last but not least, defining how to measure success after rollout.

# Lifecycle

<br />

![](/sally-lifecycle.jpg)

<!--
Let’s give Sally a closer look.

The lifecycle revolves around three agents: Refiner, Planner, and Coder.

The journey starts with a business analyst creating a backlog item, typically of the poorly written kind.

The developer asks the Refiner for help.

The Refiner retrieves the backlog item and refines it.

The developer reviews the document, either interacting with the agent or editing it directly, until it meets expectations.

This is the first quality gate. The developer must explicitly approve the refined backlog item before proceeding to the next stage.

Then the Planner creates an implementation plan.

Once again, the developer is responsible for quality. They review and revise the plan until they are ready to approve it.

Once we’re past this second quality gate, the developer invokes the Coder.

The Coder is responsible for the implementation and the tests.

And for the third time, the developer is responsible for quality.

They keep interacting with the agent until the implementation meets the expectations and quality standards.

When all the boxes are checked, the developer approves and asks the Coder to create the pull request. And after that comes the peer code review.

So, you see how human hands remain firmly on the steering wheel throughout the lifecycle.

Each agent comes with specific **skills** (such as retrieving the backlog item, querying the code graph, or creating the pull request) and with guidelines and **templates** for producing the artifacts passed from one stage to the next.

-->

# Code graph

- In June the costs of AI skyrocketed
- Search the huge codebase vs. query a structured representation of the codebase and its relationships
- Two custom ingestion pipelines
- One common graph representation stored in Neo4j
- Different streams → different branches → different graphs
- Multiple disposable graph databases on one Neo4j instance in the cloud
- Custom MCP server + skill

<!--

Now, we introduced the first version of this Sally-like lifecycle at the customer in April-May, which was not really timely because in June the costs of AI skyrocketed.

That was not ideal, especially when agents needed to repeatedly search the huge codebase to discover how things worked and how to make changes.

That’s one of the main reasons why we introduced the graph. Instead of repeatedly searching the source code, agents could query a structured representation of the codebase and its relationships.

Tools like CodeGraphContext were not vetted in that environment and would have been neither easy nor fast to adopt, so we built two custom ingestion pipelines.

One analyzed the .NET backend using Roslyn. Another analyzed the React frontend. Both produced a common graph representation stored in Neo4j.

In this team, different streams worked on different branches, so we needed several versions of that graph.

We ended up maintaining multiple disposable graph databases on one Neo4j instance in the cloud, rebuilding them from the relevant branches on a schedule.

Then we put a small custom MCP server in front of Neo4j and gave the agents a skill that taught them how to query the graph associated with the relevant branch.

-->

# Developer feedback

- “It reduced my development time by about 50%.”
- “It helped me reduce the time by 30 or 40%.”
- “The first implementation was around 70% good.”
- “A lot of the specification was in attachments… it skipped that scope.”
- “We really have to make it see the images.”
- “For small bugs, it feels like overkill.”
- The importance of <span v-mark="{ at: 0, color: '#e9562040', type: 'highlight' }">choosing the right model</span>

<!--

Developers consistently saw the strongest value before coding even started. In refinement, planning, understanding the domain, and getting to a useful first implementation.

One developer estimated their development time had been reduced by about 50%.

Another described the first implementation as roughly 70% good. Not something you would merge blindly, obviously, but a very useful starting point.

Areas for improvement emerged as well.

When important requirements lived in attachments or visual material the agents could not access, they simply missed part of the scope.

Developers also realized that, for small changes such as bug fixes, going through the entire lifecycle felt like overkill.

And feedback taught us something else about cost.

Using the most capable and expensive models for every step made little sense.

We improved cost efficiency by using expensive models where reasoning mattered most, especially during refinement and planning, and cheaper, “dumber” models for coding, especially when the implementation plan was already strong enough to constrain the work.

-->

# Practical risks

- Lock a bunch of agents in a dark room and hope they behave?! 😱
- A factory where nobody really knows what’s happening inside their manufacturing plants?! 😱
- Use AI ethically, responsibly, and with humans being accountable for the outcome
- Think of medicine and transportation. Regulation doesn’t make them risk-free, but...

<!--

You can put all the guardrails you want in place, but I still don’t believe the answer is to lock a bunch of agents in a dark room and hope they behave.

And I honestly wonder why an organization would want a factory where nobody really knows what’s happening inside their manufacturing plants.

Call me old-fashioned. Or just old. Anxious. A control freak. Whatever. But I will never stop recommending that we use AI ethically, responsibly, and with humans being accountable for the outcome.

After all, we regulate all sorts of things. Think of medicine and transportation. Regulation doesn’t make them risk-free, but when technology can cause real harm, we don’t simply rely on people behaving sensibly. I don’t see why increasingly autonomous AI systems should be treated differently.

-->
